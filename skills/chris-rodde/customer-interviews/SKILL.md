# Customer interviews for B2B marketing

Interviews are the evidence layer under positioning, messaging, ICP and growth decisions. Most interview programs fail in one of three places: they talk to the wrong people, they ask questions people can't answer truthfully, or the findings never become a decision. This skill exists to prevent all three.

It combines four practitioners. Each covers a gap the others leave:

| Practitioner | What they contribute | Where it shows up |
|---|---|---|
| **Georgiana Laudi & Claire Suellentrop** (Forget the Funnel, now Customer Led) | Marketing-specific JTBD research on *best* customers; translating interviews into messaging and the customer journey | Study design, the best-customer guide, synthesis |
| **Teresa Torres** (Product Talk, *Continuous Discovery Habits*) | Story-based interviewing; separating research questions from interview questions; the interview snapshot | Guide design, interview technique, per-interview synthesis |
| **Rob Fitzpatrick** (*The Mom Test*) | Rules that stop people lying to you politely; how to deflect compliments and dig under feature requests | Question hygiene, interviewer rubric |
| **Clozd** (Andrew Peterson, Braydon Anderson and team) | Win-loss and churn interviews with buyers; buying-committee and competitor coverage; interviews vs. surveys | Win-loss/churn guides, timing, scale |

Bob Moesta's Four Forces is the analysis lens. A switch happens only when **push** (frustration with the current situation) plus **pull** (attraction of the new solution) outweighs **anxiety** (what might go wrong if they switch) plus **habit** (the pull of what they already do). Teams over-invest in pull; anxiety and habit rarely surface unless the interviewer asks.

Read `references/practitioners.md` for each method in more depth and for sources.

## The sequence

| Step | Question | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Decide | What decision will this research inform? | Research brief |
| 2. Select | Who has the evidence, and how do we reach them? | Participant criteria, screener, invite |
| 3. Design | What story do we collect to answer our research questions? | Interview guide |
| 4. Run | Are we getting real stories, or polite opinions? | Recordings, transcripts |
| 5. Score | Was each interview reliable, and what did it say? | Interview quality score, coded transcript, snapshot |
| 6. Synthesize | What patterns hold across interviews, and what do they rule out? | Findings report or demand brief |

Announce which step you're on. Each step narrows the next. A study that starts at step 3 ("give me interview questions") almost always has an unstated decision and unchosen participants. Ask the step 1 and 2 questions briefly before drafting, unless the user has already answered them.

## Step 1 — Decide

Read `references/study-design.md`.

Get one sentence: **"We are deciding ___, and we'll know enough when ___."** Examples: which churning segment to fight for; what the homepage should lead with; why we lose to a specific competitor.

Then set the **interview type**, because it changes everything downstream:

- **Best-customer / switch interview** — recent, happy, high-value customers. Why they went looking, what they compared, what made them choose, what value they now get. Default for messaging and positioning.
- **Win-loss interview** — buyers who recently decided, for or against. What prompted the evaluation, who was involved, how vendors compared, what decided it.
- **Churn interview** — customers who recently cancelled or downgraded. The forces in reverse.
- **Non-switcher interview** — people who evaluated and stayed put. Best source of anxiety and habit.

Write 3–5 **research questions** (what you need to learn). These are never asked aloud. Torres's distinction is the core of this skill: research questions go in the brief; interview questions collect stories that answer them.

## Step 2 — Select

Read `references/study-design.md`.

- **Who:** for best-customer studies, use Laudi's criteria — happily paying, reached value without hand-holding, urgent problem, retained or expanded. For win-loss and churn, recency beats seniority: the person closest to the decision, contacted within about two weeks of it (Clozd).
- **How many:** 10–12 per segment usually reaches pattern saturation (Laudi & Suellentrop). Count per segment, not per study.
- **Mix:** weight toward the segment the decision is about. Include losses and churns when the decision involves winning someone back.
- **Survey first?** With a large pool of qualified customers (Laudi uses roughly 500 as the line), a short survey identifies who to interview and sizes themes. Below that, go straight to interviews.

Draft the screener and invite from the templates in the reference. Never pitch in the invite.

## Step 3 — Design the guide

Read `references/interview-guide.md`.

A guide is not a questionnaire. It is one or two story prompts plus the probes that excavate the story, organized along the timeline of a decision. Build it like this:

1. Translate each research question into the **story** that would answer it. Usually one story covers several: "Tell me about the last time you…" or "Take me back to when you first started looking for…"
2. Lay out the **timeline** the story should cover (first thought → looking → deciding → first use → value now, or the win-loss/churn equivalent).
3. Add **probes** for each phase, drawn from the question bank, that pull specifics: dates, people, artifacts, alternatives, what almost stopped them.
4. Add **must-cover topics** (Clozd's principle 2) for anything that needs to be comparable across interviews — competitors considered, pricing reaction, buying committee.
5. Move anything that needs a **rating** to a short post-interview survey (Clozd: if it needs a rating it's a survey item; if it needs a story it's an interview item).
6. Run every question through the **Mom Test check**: about their life, not our idea; about a specific past, not general or hypothetical; short enough that they talk more than we do.

Target 30–45 minutes for customers, 30 minutes or less for win-loss buyers. Score the draft against the Guide Quality rubric before handing it over.

## Step 4 — Run

Read the "In the room" section of `references/interview-guide.md`. Key rules:

- Open with context and permission, not your product.
- Collect the story chronologically and stay in it. When the participant generalizes ("usually we…"), return to the specific instance ("in that case, what happened?").
- Let them go where they care most — Torres's golden rule — but probe what's influential, and skip what isn't (Clozd's adaptation principle).
- Deflect compliments, anchor fluff, dig under feature requests (Fitzpatrick).
- Never defend the product or correct the participant. Write it down.
- Record and transcribe. The exact words are the asset.

## Step 5 — Score

Read `references/rubrics.md`. Four rubrics, each with anchored 1–4 scales:

| Rubric | Use it to | When |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Participant fit** | Screen and prioritize who to interview | Before scheduling |
| **B. Guide quality** | Review a draft guide | Before the first interview |
| **C. Interview quality** | Judge how reliable one interview's evidence is, and coach the interviewer | After each interview |
| **D. Evidence coding** | Code what the transcript says: forces, timeline, alternatives, language | After each interview |

When the user asks to "draft a rubric," ask which job it's for if the context doesn't make it obvious, then **tailor** the matching template to their study: replace generic anchors with the specific behaviors, competitors, triggers and segments of their market. A generic rubric is a starting point, not a deliverable.

Interview quality (C) gates synthesis: evidence from interviews scoring below 2 on story specificity or leading-question avoidance is labeled weak and never carries a finding alone.

## Step 6 — Synthesize

Read `references/synthesis.md`.

- Write an **interview snapshot** for each interview the same day (Torres). Synthesize one at a time, not in a batch at the end.
- Code each transcript with Rubric D into a shared sheet: one row per interview, columns for trigger, alternatives, forces, desired outcome, verbatims.
- Look for patterns across interviews: struggle, motivation and desired-outcome themes (Laudi & Suellentrop), and Four Forces by segment.
- Report **frequency honestly** ("7 of 11 named…"), separate what was seen from what is inferred, and state what the evidence **rules out**.
- Hand off: for messaging work, produce a demand brief (struggling moment, Four Forces, alternatives, language inventory) ready to drive the positioning decision; for win-loss, a decision-driver table with competitor patterns; for churn, a by-segment reason map.

## Standing guardrails

- **Research questions asked aloud.** "What features matter most to you?" invites a fast, plausible, unreliable answer. Collect a story instead.
- **Hypotheticals.** "Would you use…" and "How much would you pay…" measure politeness. Ask what they did and paid.
- **Pitching.** Once the interviewer describes the product, the rest of the interview is contaminated. Save demos for after, if at all.
- **Interviewing the convenient.** Champions, friendly logos and whoever answers email first produce a flattering, useless sample.
- **The interviewer has a stake.** A salesperson interviewing their own lost deal gets a polite story. Use someone neutral for win-loss and churn where possible, and note who interviewed in the findings.
- **One loud interview.** Aggregate before concluding. A vivid quote is not a pattern.
- **Cleaning the language.** Keep verbatims verbatim. The customer's word for the problem is the copy.
- **Findings that change nothing.** Every study ends with what it means for the decision named in step 1.
- **Privacy.** Get recording consent, store transcripts where the participant was told they'd be, and anonymize quotes by default (role and company type) unless the participant clears attribution.

## What good looks like

- **The first thing to check is the decision, not the questions.** A study with a named decision and an evidence threshold produces findings someone acts on. A study that starts with "give me interview questions" produces a themes deck nobody uses.
- **Transcripts read like stories, not surveys.** Dates, names, the spreadsheet they used before, the meeting where someone pushed back. If most answers start with "usually" or "I think," the interviewer asked opinion questions and the evidence is weak.
- **Anxiety and habit show up in the coding.** The mediocre study captures only push and pull — what frustrated people and what attracted them. The good one also names what nearly stopped the switch, because that is what the funnel and the copy have to dismantle.
- **Findings carry counts and limits.** "7 of 11 named the migration as the reason they delayed" beats "customers worry about migration." Every finding says which segment it holds for and what it rules out.
- **The participant does nearly all the talking.** A strong interview has the interviewer under 15–30% of talk time. Above 30% almost always means pitching, leading or answering for them.
- **Verbatims survive intact into the hand-off.** The customer's exact phrase for the problem is the most valuable output for messaging.
- **Sample size is honest.** 10–12 interviews per segment reaches pattern saturation. Fewer is fine for a directional read, as long as the report says so.

## Rules

- MUST name the decision the study informs before drafting any interview question.
- MUST write research questions separately from interview questions, and never ask research questions aloud.
- MUST get recording consent before recording, and anonymize quotes by default.
- MUST score each interview for reliability before its evidence carries a finding; evidence scoring below 2 on story specificity or leading-question avoidance never carries a finding alone.
- NEVER ask hypotheticals ("would you…", "how much would you pay…") or pitch the product during an interview.
- NEVER clean up or paraphrase verbatims presented as customer language.
- NEVER report a pattern from a single interview, however vivid.

## Output templates

### Research brief (end of step 1)
```
Decision this informs — one sentence
We'll know enough when — the evidence threshold
Interview type — best-customer / win-loss / churn / non-switcher
Research questions — 3–5, never asked aloud
Segments — and target interviews per segment
Out of scope — what this study will not answer
Owner, interviewer(s), dates
```

### Interview guide (end of step 3)
```
Header — study, interview type, length, interviewer, consent script
Warm-up — role, context (2–3 min)
Story prompt — the one or two questions that open the story
Timeline probes — by phase
Must-cover topics — for cross-interview comparability
Close — anything we didn't ask, who else to talk to, thanks
Post-interview survey — any rated items
Research-question map — which probes answer which research question
```
